# Compliance review of audit-trail-writer hash chain against 21 CFR Part 11

The compliance review concluded that the hash chain of audit-trail-writer satisfies clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11` on secure, time-stamped audit trails. This note records what that conclusion covers, what it rests on, what it does not cover, and how to use it later. It is a working note, not the formal report. If the formal report and this note disagree, the formal report wins and this note should be fixed.

The short version for someone in a hurry: the review looked at whether audit-trail-writer produces records that are computer-generated, time-stamped, tamper-evident, and kept so that earlier entries are not overwritten when something changes. The hash chain is the mechanism that carries most of that argument. The reviewers accepted it for `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11`. They did not sign off on every other clause of the regulation, and they did not sign off on the whole product. Anyone quoting this review in an audit should quote the scope as narrowly as it was given.

## What was concluded

The finding is that the hash chain design in audit-trail-writer meets the intent and the wording of clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11`. That clause is about secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails that record the date and time of operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete electronic records. It also says that record changes must not obscure previously recorded information, and that the audit trail documentation must be kept for at least as long as the subject electronic records and be available for agency review and copying.

The reviewers broke the clause into the following questions and answered each from the design and from evidence supplied by the team.

- Is the audit trail generated by the system and not typed by a person? Yes. Entries are produced by audit-trail-writer from events it receives, and no user-facing screen lets someone compose an audit entry by hand.
- Is each entry time-stamped from a source the operator cannot set? Yes, with a caveat about clock handling that is covered below under open points.
- Does a change to a record leave the earlier state visible? Yes. Changes are appended as new entries that refer back to what came before. Nothing is edited in place.
- Can tampering with a stored entry be detected? Yes. Each entry carries a hash that covers its own content and the hash of the entry before it, so altering, removing, or reordering an entry breaks the chain from that point forward.
- Is the trail retained and reproducible for inspection? The reviewers accepted the retention approach, which relies on long-term storage in Azure Blob Storage and on the relational store, and they accepted that the trail can be exported in a readable form.

The word the reviewers kept coming back to was evidence. A design that is sound on paper is not enough for a compliance officer, so the conclusion is tied to verification runs, sample exports, and a walk-through of the failure cases. Those are described in the next section.

## What the review looked at

The review covered the writer component and the pieces it depends on directly. It did not try to review the whole synchronization path from instruments to notebook entries. The scope was set so that the question being answered was narrow enough to answer properly.

### Components in scope

audit-trail-writer is a C# service on .NET. It receives audit events, builds the entry, computes the hash that links the entry to its predecessor, and persists it. Events arrive over RabbitMQ from the other parts of LabNotebook Sync. Persistence goes to SQL Server for the live, queryable trail, and sealed segments of the trail are copied out to Azure Blob Storage for long-term retention. The review looked at all four touch points: the queue intake, the hashing step, the SQL Server write, and the archive step.

The notebook-side components that decide what counts as an auditable event were treated as inputs. The reviewers asked whether audit-trail-writer could be bypassed by them, and concluded that the intended design does not allow it. They did not review the correctness of those components themselves. If an upstream component fails to emit an event for a change, the hash chain cannot help, because it only protects what it was given. This is the single most important limit on the conclusion and it is repeated in the open points.

### Evidence the reviewers used

The evidence fell into four groups.

First, design material. The team supplied a description of the entry structure, the ordering rule, and the way the hash is computed. The reviewers read the description against the code and checked that the two matched. Where the description was vague, they asked for clarification and the description was updated before the conclusion was recorded.

Second, code reading. A compliance-minded engineer and a second reviewer read the hashing and persistence code. They looked for any path that writes an entry without chaining it, any path that updates or deletes an existing entry, and any place where a caller could supply its own time stamp or its own hash. They found no such path in the writer. They did note a few places where the code was harder to follow than it should be, and those became cleanup items rather than findings.

Third, verification runs. The team ran the chain verifier against a populated trail and confirmed that it reported the chain as intact. They then deliberately altered a copy of the data in several ways and confirmed that the verifier caught each alteration and pointed at the first broken entry. The alterations included changing the content of an entry, removing an entry from the middle, swapping two adjacent entries, and truncating the tail. Truncation of the tail is the hardest case for any hash chain, because a shorter chain is still internally consistent. The review covers how that case is handled in the section on limits below.

Fourth, sample exports. The reviewers asked for exports of the trail for a chosen set of notebook records and checked that the exports show who did what, when, to which record, and what the earlier value was. They checked that the export is readable by someone who is not an engineer, since a compliance officer or an inspector is the real audience.

## Why the hash chain is accepted

This section is the reasoning, written down so it does not have to be reconstructed next time.

### How the chain works, in plain terms

Every audit entry is a record with the acting user, the action, the target record, the before and after information, and a time stamp. audit-trail-writer builds a canonical form of that content, combines it with the hash of the immediately preceding entry, and hashes the result. The new hash is stored with the entry. The next entry will include this hash in its own computation. So each entry commits to everything before it, and a change anywhere in history changes every later hash.

Because of this, there is no way to quietly edit an old entry. An attacker, or a careless administrator with database access, would have to recompute every later hash, and would also have to make the stored copy in long-term storage agree. The archive copy is the reason the second step is hard: sealed segments are written out and the hash at the end of each segment is recorded in a place that does not share a permission boundary with the live database. A person who can edit SQL Server rows does not automatically get to edit the archived segment.

### Why this answers the clause

Clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11` asks for audit trails that are secure and that do not obscure earlier information. The reviewers read secure as meaning that the trail resists undetected alteration, not that it is impossible to touch at the storage layer. No system can promise that nobody with infrastructure access could ever do anything. What a regulated system can promise is that unauthorized changes are detected and that the detection does not depend on the honesty of the person who made the change. The hash chain gives that property, and the independent archive of segment boundaries strengthens it.

The append-only behavior answers the part about not obscuring earlier information. An update to a notebook record becomes a new audit entry that carries the prior state. The earlier entry stays where it was and still verifies. A reader following the chain sees the whole history in order.

The time stamp question was handled by looking at where the time comes from. The time is taken by the writer when it processes the event, from the server clock, and not from the client. The event may also carry the time the originating action took place, and the entry stores both so that a delay in the queue is visible rather than hidden. The reviewers liked that the two are kept separate, because it avoids the argument over whether the time stamp reflects when the action happened or when it was recorded.

### What the reviewers pushed on

The discussion was not one-sided. Several questions took real time before the reviewers were satisfied.

Ordering under concurrency was the first. If two events arrive at nearly the same moment, which one comes first in the chain? The writer serializes the chain extension so that each entry is chained to exactly one predecessor, and the order is the order in which the writer commits them. The reviewers accepted that the order of the chain is the authoritative order of the trail, even if it differs slightly from the order in which the events were produced upstream. The stored original action time lets a reader see any such difference.

Redelivery from RabbitMQ was the second. Message brokers can deliver the same message more than once. If the writer chained duplicates, the trail would contain false repeats, and a reader could think an action happened twice. The reviewers checked how the writer recognizes a repeat and drops it without breaking the chain, and they checked the opposite case, where a message is lost before it is acknowledged and then comes back. They concluded that the intake handles both, with the caveat that the identification of a repeat relies on an identifier carried by the event, so upstream components must keep that identifier stable.

The choice of hash function was the third. The reviewers confirmed that the function used is a current, widely accepted one and that it is not a home-made construction. They also asked whether the design allows the function to be changed in the future without invalidating the old trail. The answer is that each segment records which function produced it, so a later change would start a new segment under the new function and the old segments would continue to verify under the old one. This was accepted as adequate. Nobody needs to act on it now.

Canonicalization was the fourth. A hash is only as stable as the bytes fed into it. If the same logical entry could be serialized in two different ways, verification could fail for innocent reasons or an attacker could exploit the ambiguity. The reviewers checked that the canonical form has a fixed field order, fixed text encoding, and fixed number and date formats, and that the verifier uses the same routine as the writer. A shared routine is good, and also a risk, because a bug in it would be present on both sides and not noticed. The team added independent test vectors so that the canonical form is pinned by examples and not only by the code that generates it.

## Limits of the conclusion and open points

This section matters more than the one above if you are about to cite the review. The conclusion is real, but it is narrow.

### Scope limits

The conclusion is about clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11` and about audit-trail-writer. It is not a statement that LabNotebook Sync as a whole is compliant with `21 CFR Part 11`. Other clauses cover things such as electronic signatures, access controls, system validation, operational checks, and training, and those were not part of this review. If a customer asks whether the product is compliant, the accurate answer is that this one clause was reviewed for this one component and found satisfied, and that the other areas are handled separately.

The conclusion is also about the design and the evidence available at review time. It does not freeze the code. A later change to the entry structure, the hashing step, the ordering rule, or the archive step can invalidate the argument, and a change of that kind should trigger a short re-review of the affected part. Changes that are clearly outside those areas, such as logging format or deployment tweaks, do not need it.

### Completeness depends on upstream

The hash chain proves that what was recorded has not been changed. It does not prove that everything that should have been recorded was recorded. If an upstream component changes a notebook entry or accepts instrument output without emitting an audit event, the trail has a hole that no hash can reveal. The reviewers flagged this clearly. The mitigation they accepted is that the paths that modify records go through shared code that emits the event as part of the same operation, and that a reconciliation check compares record changes with audit entries. The reconciliation check was demonstrated but not reviewed in depth. Treat it as a recommended control and not as part of the finding.

### Tail truncation

A chain can be cut at the end and the remainder still verifies. The reviewers wanted a defense and the team already had one: the end hash of each sealed segment is recorded in the archive, and the live tail is anchored by periodic sealing. So a cut inside a sealed segment is caught, and a cut in the live tail can only remove a short, recent stretch, which the sealing cadence bounds. The reviewers accepted this but noted that the residual window exists. If a stricter reading is demanded by a customer or an inspector, the answer is to seal more often, which costs little. This was left as a tuning choice and not as a defect.

### Clock handling

The time stamp is only as trustworthy as the server clock. The reviewers asked how clock drift and manual clock changes are handled. The expected operating practice is that servers synchronize to a trusted time source and that unusual jumps are alarmed. The writer also refuses to produce an entry whose time stamp is earlier than that of its predecessor without recording the anomaly, so a clock moving backward becomes visible in the trail and not silently reordered. The reviewers accepted this as sufficient for the clause, and they asked that the operating guidance state the time synchronization expectation plainly. That wording is a documentation task.

### Privileged access

A database administrator can do things to SQL Server that the application never would. The review does not claim otherwise. It claims that such actions on the live trail are detectable after the fact, because the chain and the archived segment boundaries would disagree. It does not claim that they are prevented. Reducing who holds that access, and logging what they do at the platform level, is a procedural control for the operating team and is outside the code. The reviewers recommended that the operating procedures say this out loud, since an inspector is likely to ask.

### Key and secret handling

The chain as described does not depend on a secret key, which keeps things simple, but it also means that anyone who can recompute hashes and who controls both copies of the data could in principle rebuild a consistent forged history. The independent archive and the separate permission boundary are what stand between that scenario and reality. The reviewers did not ask for a keyed construction at this time. They did note that if the threat model grows to include an attacker with control of both stores, a keyed or externally anchored design would be worth considering. That is a possible future improvement, not a gap against the clause.

## What to do with this review

Some practical guidance for the next person who touches this.

When a customer or inspector asks about audit trails, point to this conclusion and state its scope in the same breath: audit-trail-writer, the hash chain, clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11`. Do not generalize it. If the question is about signatures or access control, this note has nothing to say.

When changing audit-trail-writer, ask whether the change touches the entry structure, canonical form, ordering, hashing, intake deduplication, or archive sealing. If yes, tell the compliance officers before merging, and expect to repeat the relevant part of the verification: run the verifier on a populated trail, then on deliberately altered copies, and look at a sample export. If the change is elsewhere in the service, a normal review is enough.

When adding a new upstream feature that changes records, make sure it goes through the shared path that emits the audit event. The chain cannot protect what it never sees. A new feature that writes directly to the notebook tables without an event is the most likely way for this conclusion to become untrue without anyone noticing.

When changing the queue setup in RabbitMQ, keep in mind that the intake depends on stable event identifiers and on acknowledgement behavior. Changes to retry or redelivery policy, or to how messages are routed to the writer, are worth a second look from someone who has read the deduplication logic.

When changing storage, the archive in Azure Blob Storage is part of the argument and not just a backup. Moving it to a location that shares permissions with the live database weakens the case for tamper evidence. Lifecycle or retention policy changes on the archive should be checked against the retention expectation in the clause, which is that the trail lives at least as long as the records it describes.

### Follow-ups the review left behind

These are items the reviewers asked for. None of them changes the conclusion, but they should be tracked.

- Write down the time synchronization expectation in the operating guidance.
- State in the operating procedures that privileged database access is detectable and not prevented, and describe how detection is done.
- Keep the independent test vectors for the canonical form alongside the code, and run them in the normal build.
- Consider sealing the live tail more often if a stricter reading of the truncation risk is ever demanded.
- Review the reconciliation check that compares record changes with audit entries, since it is what covers the completeness gap.
- Clean up the harder-to-follow spots in the hashing and persistence code that the code readers pointed out, without altering behavior, and re-run the verification afterward.

## Notes on how the review was run

This is here mostly so the next review can be run the same way, or better.

The reviewers started from the clause text and worked toward the code, not the other way around. Starting from the code tends to produce a list of things the code does, and then a hunt for a clause that fits. Starting from the clause produced a list of questions that the code had to answer, and it made the gaps, such as completeness and time source, show up early.

The team supplied a short, readable description of the design before the reviewers looked at code. That saved time, and when the description and the code disagreed, the disagreement itself was informative. In two places the description was out of date, and those were fixed. Keep the description current, since it is the first thing the next reviewer will read.

The deliberate tampering exercise was the most convincing piece of evidence for the compliance officers. A statement that tampering is detected is easy to doubt. Watching the verifier flag the first broken entry after an edit, a removal, a swap, and a truncation is much harder to doubt. If the exercise is repeated, record the steps in a form that a non-engineer can follow and keep the output.

The sample exports were reviewed by someone who does not write code, which turned out to matter. Several column names and wording choices that were obvious to engineers were unclear to a compliance reader. The team adjusted the labels. A trail that cannot be read by the people who must rely on it does not do its job, however sound the hashing is.

Finally, the reviewers kept a clear line between a finding and a recommendation. The finding is that the hash chain of audit-trail-writer satisfies clause `11.10(e)` of `21 CFR Part 11`. Everything in the follow-up list is a recommendation that improves the position or documents the operating context. Mixing the two would make the conclusion sound conditional when it is not, or would make the recommendations sound mandatory when they are not. Keep them apart when you quote this review.

## If the conclusion is challenged

Expect two kinds of challenge, and answer each in turn.

The first is a challenge to the mechanism: someone argues that a hash chain is not enough, or that it can be rebuilt. The answer is the reasoning in the section on why the chain is accepted. The chain makes alteration detectable, the independent archive of segment boundaries makes a consistent rebuild hard, and the clause asks for secure and non-obscuring trails, which this provides. If the challenger has a specific attack in mind, take it seriously and check whether it requires control of both stores. If it does, that is the privileged access and key handling discussion above, and the honest answer is that it is a residual risk handled by procedure.

The second is a challenge to the scope: someone argues that the review claims too much. Here the answer is to restate the scope exactly. One component, one clause. Nothing in this note says the product as a whole is compliant with `21 CFR Part 11`, and nothing should be read that way. If the formal report words the scope differently from this note, use the formal report.

If a real defect is found later, such as a path that writes an entry without chaining it, or a way to alter an entry without detection, treat the conclusion as suspended for that part until it is fixed and re-verified. Tell the compliance officers at once. Do not wait for the next scheduled review. Then update this note with what was found, what changed, and what evidence now supports the conclusion, and remove any statement that no longer holds.
